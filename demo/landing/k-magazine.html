The Craft

Most frontend problems are not framework problems. They are decisions nobody wrote down — about rendering, images, and who is allowed to add a script tag.

Words by Harshal Singh · Frontend Engineer · Mumbai

When a marketing page takes four or five seconds to become usable, you lose trust before anyone reads a word. That was the state of the site I inherited: client-side rendering, oversized hero images, and a CDN doing close to nothing. The brief was simple — make it fast, make it Google-friendly, ship it in a quarter.

Rendering, per route

The biggest architectural win was treating rendering as a per-route decision rather than a global default. Marketing pages moved to static generation with incremental revalidation. The blog revalidates hourly. Internal dashboards stayed client-side, where they belong. Nothing about that is clever; it is just refusing to apply one strategy to every page.

Images beat JavaScript

Most regressions on Next.js sites come from images, not bundles. AVIF output, responsive sizes, and priority loading on the LCP candidate beat almost any JavaScript optimisation you can attempt afterwards. Hero images went from 1.2MB JPEGs to roughly 180KB AVIF, and the lab LCP dropped 35% off the back of that single change.

A CMS without a CMS vendor

Content lived in Google Sheets. That sounds like a compromise and occasionally it is, but it let editors work in a tool they already understood while the site stayed fully static at the edge. Schema versioning and seed fallbacks keep it running even when a tab is empty. Editor onboarding fell from two weeks to three days.

Shipping is a pipeline problem

None of it matters if releases are painful. Docker images and an Azure DevOps pipeline took deploys from eighteen minutes to under nine, which changed how often anyone was willing to push. Speed of delivery is a design constraint, not an afterthought.

The through-line across three years and three teams is the same: find the decision nobody made explicit, make it explicit, then measure whether it helped.

Treating rendering as a per-route decision — not a global default — was the biggest architectural win.
